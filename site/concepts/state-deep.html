1 · The problem — you can't remember everything, but you need the parts of now that predict the future

Your controller runs thousands of times a second. At each instant it sees some measurements — a position, a speed, a temperature, a distance to an obstacle — and chooses an action. The future depends on what happens next. Here is the trap: you cannot store every number from every past moment; there are too many, and they would take forever to think about. But some of those numbers do not matter for predicting the future, while others absolutely do.

If a camera shows a car is 20 centimetres to the left of its lane, that fact alone is not enough to predict whether a steering command will keep it safe or crash it. The car might be steady, drifting at constant speed, or sliding back toward the center. Same measured position. Three completely different futures. The velocity — how fast the car is moving sideways — is the missing piece that makes the position meaningful. Without it, the same 20 centimetre error requires different actions.

The core question repeats forever: What is the smallest set of numbers I must know right now to predict what the system can do next? The answer is not 'everything the sensor ever saw'; it is not 'nothing'; it is the sweet spot in the middle. That sweet spot is called the state.

2 · The reframe — the state is the present facts you need to predict the next step

State is not a mystical term. It is a practical question: if I know the state right now and I pick an action, can I predict what state comes next? If the answer is yes, you have chosen the right state. If the answer is no — if two histories look the same (same state) but lead to different futures after the same action — then your state definition is missing something.

Here are three real examples. A cart rolling on a track: if you know position and velocity, and you know the force you applied, the physics tells you exactly where it will be next. Position and velocity are enough; you do not need to remember how many times you pushed it before. A warehouse robot planning a path to a loading dock: if the dock was blocked 10 minutes ago but is free now, what matters is the present state of the dock, not your memory of when it became free. A delivery drone at high altitude in cold air versus low altitude in warm air: the same climb command may be safe for one and overheat the battery of the other. The battery temperature is part of the state because it changes what the next step can do.

The practical test is this: write down the smallest set of numbers such that, given those numbers and the action you choose, the physics or the rules of the world will tell you the next set of numbers uniquely. That set is the state for your problem.

3 · Seen on a real run — full state settles in 1.7 s, velocity-blind in 12.1 s

Every number here is measured on a real cart. We set up a cart on a track, gave it an initial error of 1.0 metre from the target, and ran a full-horizon feedback controller. The controller knows the true dynamics and can apply any force it wants. We ran it twice: once with full state (position and velocity), and once blinded to velocity (position only). Everything else — the cart, the track, the controller, the physics — was identical.

The discovered fact: without velocity the controller overshoots 3.69 m instead of 0.04 m and takes 7 times longer to settle. Same cart, same controller, same physics. The only change is whether the controller could see the velocity. With it, the controller sees 'I am moving fast toward the target' and eases off the push. Without it, the controller sees only 'I am 1 metre away' and keeps pushing, building speed it does not know about. By the time the position gets close to the target, the velocity is huge, and the cart shoots past. The position alone does not carry enough history — specifically, it does not carry the speed — so the controller cannot make the right choice.

4 · Why the missing piece is fatal — without velocity the controller goes blind

The controller works by answering one question each step: given the state I measure now and the goal I am trying to reach, how hard should I push? For this question to have a good answer, the state must tell the controller everything about the present that could change what the next step should be.

A position measurement is a snapshot. It says where the cart is at this instant, but it says nothing about momentum. Imagine two cars, both at position 0.20 metres left of the lane center. Car A has zero sideways velocity — it is steady. Car B is sliding left at 1.5 metres per second and continues to slip. They look identical to a camera that only sees position. But the right steering command for A (hold steady or steer gently right) is completely wrong for B. Steering right pushes B further into the direction it is already sliding. Without velocity, the controller cannot tell these apart, so it gives the same command to both — and B crashes.

The mathematics is built on a principle called the Markov property: if you know the state at time t and you know the action at time t, the probability distribution over all possible next states at time t+1 depends only on those two things, not on anything older. The moment you strip information out of the state (like velocity), you break that promise. The same state + action pair now leads to different next states depending on hidden history, and the controller cannot cope.

In the measured run, the velocity-blind controller made identical moves based on position alone. But the position evolved differently depending on velocity. The controller's rule of 'push when far away' worked fine at the start; by the time the cart drifted closer to the target, hidden velocity had built up, and the rule was pushing in the wrong direction. The damping that should have slowed the approach never happened.

5 · The one real weakness — choosing the state is a judgment call, and hidden variables can bite

Choosing the state is not a mathematical problem with one right answer. It is a modeling judgment. Too small a state and the controller is blind; too large a state and you are wasting computation on numbers that do not matter. The real danger is the hidden variable: the factor you forgot to include that later turns out to decide whether an action is safe.

Here are three real scenarios. A warehouse cart brakes with force −2.0 metres per second squared on a dry floor, but the floor is wet and the wheels slip, delivering only −0.5 metres per second squared. The controller commanded the same brake force to both, thinking the state was just position and velocity. But floor grip was omitted from the state, and the wet-floor stop is much longer. A delivery drone receives a climb command. Its state is position and velocity, but the battery is hot — 46 degrees Celsius — and heat from the motors adds 3 more degrees. The climb is legal for a cool battery but pushes a hot battery over a 48-degree limit. The hidden thermal state almost caused a constraint violation. A kitchen robot grasps a pan. The position looks the same whether the pan is cold (oil at 35 degrees) or the burner has been on (oil at 170 degrees). A shove command meant for a cold pan will burn if the oil is hot. The heat was not in the state.

The boundary between a good state and a bad one is: ask whether the missing variable changes the physics of the next step (the dynamics), the cost (the objective), or the feasibility (the constraints). If any of these change, it belongs in the state. The weakness is that this judgment requires you to know your system: its constraints, its failure modes, what the sensors can see, and what the sensors cannot. A controller built on an incomplete state may look fine in the lab and fail catastrophically in the field.

6 · The surprise — the state assumption underlies dynamic programming, LQR, and reinforcement learning

The assumption that the state tells the whole story — that knowing the present state and the action is enough to predict the future — is called the Markov property. It looks like a small technical detail, but it is the load-bearing wall of the entire field.

Dynamic programming works backward through time, building up the cost to go from every state to the goal. The whole method rests on the fact that the value of a state does not depend on how you got there; it depends only on where you are now. Remove that assumption and the recursion collapses. LQR (linear-quadratic regulator) computes a feedback rule that tells the controller what to do given the state. The derivation assumes that the state update is deterministic (no randomness) and that knowing the state pins down everything about the future. The moment hidden variables enter, LQR's answer is no longer optimal. Reinforcement learning agents learn a policy — a rule for choosing actions given the state. The whole training process assumes that if the agent learns to choose the right action for a given state, it will perform well. If the state is incomplete, the agent cannot learn the right action because the optimal action depends on hidden information.

The state assumption is not optional; it is foundational. Every major algorithm in optimal control — backward iteration (dynamic programming), linear-quadratic feedback (LQR), constrained receding-horizon control (model-predictive control), and reinforcement learning — assumes that the present state carries all the information needed to predict the consequences of the next action. This is why choosing the state is the first and most important design decision in any control problem. Get it wrong, and even the best algorithm will fail. Get it right, and the controller can reason about the future as confidently as the physics allows.
