Constraints — when you add a hard rule to a problem, the best legal answer usually costs more

1 · The problem — optimizing purely for cost can produce an illegal plan

Suppose you design a controller and the math says it is optimal — it minimises error and effort exactly the way you wrote down. You build it, place it on a real robot, and watch the motor saturate. Or the gripper clips through a shelf. Or the wheel speed exceeds what the motor can deliver. The controller is mathematically perfect. It is also not allowed.

Real systems have hard limits. A drone rotor cannot spin faster than its maximum RPM. A gripper must stay 4 centimetres from the shelf, always — not as a bonus if it is easy, but as a boundary that cannot be crossed even if crossing it would cut the energy cost in half. A motor torque has a maximum, a velocity has a peak, a fuel tank is finite. These are not trade-offs. They are the lines the plan must not step across, period.

A beginner's move is to turn a hard limit into a soft penalty: charge a small cost when a limit is violated, hope the optimizer decides it is too expensive to cross, and call it solved. This works when violations are already nearly impossible anyway — the cost curve gently pushes away from the boundary. But if breaking the rule would save a lot of energy or speed, the optimizer sees it as a trade-off, trades away, and produces a plan that is not allowed.

2 · The reframe — write the hard lines explicitly and check them before ranking any plan

A constraint is a rule that every candidate plan must pass before its cost is considered. The rule is written as an equation or inequality: the state and action must stay within a region, the gripper distance must be at least 0.04 metres, the motor force must not exceed 12 newton-metres. A candidate plan is tested against every rule at every time step. If any rule is violated, the plan is ruled out — not because it is expensive, but because it is forbidden.

Constraints split into three families by when they are checked. An initial constraint gates the very first step: the robot must start on the safe shelf, the joint must begin at the calibrated pose. A path constraint must hold at every intermediate step: the gripper stays 4 centimetres away, the motor torque stays under 12 newton-metres. A terminal constraint gates the final pose: the camera must end on the inspection target, the arm must return to rest. Clean endpoints and clean middles matter separately; you cannot forgive a missed endpoint by having a perfect middle, and a clean middle does not excuse starting wrong.

At any point in time and space, a constraint is either active (the plan is right on the boundary, using up all the allowed room) or slack (the plan is strictly inside, with margin to spare). When a constraint is slack, tiny changes to the plan do not hit the boundary. When a constraint is active, the optimizer cannot move in that direction — the boundary blocks it. This distinction matters because it tells you which constraints are really shaping the answer and which ones are just not being tested.

The discovered fact: a constraint removes options, and the best plan that still obeys it is usually slower or costlier than the one that ignores the rule. The constraint does not change the cart, the objective, or the dynamics — it only forbids the path that was fastest. The optimizer is forced to find a different trajectory that stays within the speed limit. The new trajectory settles 3.8 seconds later. This is the real price of a hard rule: not a small penalty that can be negotiated, but a substantial re-routing of the entire plan.

4 · The boundary — active versus slack tells you which rules matter

The speed limit in the run above is active during the acceleration phase: the plan is right on the boundary (1.15 m/s) and cannot go faster because the rule blocks it. Later, as the cart coasts toward the target, the plan naturally slows and the constraint becomes slack — there is margin between the plan's speed and the limit. At that point the rule is not shaping the answer; the plan would stay slow anyway.

Knowing which constraints are active tells you which rules are really doing the work. If a gripper-distance constraint is always slack by 2 centimetres, then tightening it by 1 centimetre does almost nothing to the solution. But if it is active — the plan edges right up to the 4 centimetre boundary — then tightening it to 3.5 centimetres forces a substantial re-plan. This matters for design: if a rule is never active, either the rule is too loose or it is not the bottleneck.

There is also a second kind of boundary, silent and crucial. Constraints depend on margins and uncertainty. If the nominal plan is 5 centimetres from the shelf but the shelf can flex inward by 0.8 centimetres and the camera position estimate can be wrong by 0.7 centimetres, the worst-case clearance is 5 − 0.8 − 0.7 = 3.5 centimetres. If the written rule is 'stay at least 4 centimetres away', that nominal plan is in fact unsafe: under worst-case conditions it violates the rule. A safe nominal plan must add margin to account for uncertainty. This is why engineers add 1.5-centimetre safety buffers: they force the nominal answer to stay inside a tighter written rule (5.5 centimetres instead of 4) so that even under worst-case disturbances the real system satisfies the true requirement.

5 · The one real weakness — hard constraints can make a problem infeasible

A soft penalty can always be traded: you can always save energy by accepting a small penalty. A hard constraint cannot. If the rules are too tight or contradictory, no plan can satisfy all of them. A trivial example: if you require the cart to be at position 0 and also require it to be at position 1 at the same time step, there is no legal answer. But real infeasibility is subtler. Suppose you demand that a cart go from position 0 to position 5 in exactly 0.5 seconds using only a force of at most 1 unit. That is infeasible — the physics will not allow it. The cart simply cannot accelerate fast enough. No amount of clever optimization helps; the constraint set is empty.

When a problem is infeasible, an engineer must loosen a constraint, extend the horizon, add energy budget, or accept defeat on one requirement. This is different from a soft trade-off, where the optimizer can find a middle ground. Infeasibility is binary: either a plan exists that satisfies all rules or it does not. Checking feasibility before attempting optimization is an engineering discipline. A controller that blindly tries to solve an infeasible problem produces garbage; a system that detects infeasibility and raises an alarm is design maturity. The honest weakness of constraints is that they can be incompatible, and when they are, mathematics cannot help you compromise — only engineering can, by relaxing a boundary.

6 · The surprise — this is why model-predictive control was invented

LQR computes a feedback rule offline, solving the entire problem once, and then runs that rule forever. It is fast because it does no online computation. It is fragile because it assumes perfect knowledge of the future cost and perfect linearity.

Model-predictive control (MPC) solves a small optimization problem at each time step. At time now, it predicts what the next 10 or 20 steps will cost if it takes each possible action, solves for the action sequence that minimizes that short-horizon cost, executes only the first action, and discards the rest. At the next time step, it does it all over again with fresh measurements. This re-planning step is expensive, but it is the only clean way to handle hard constraints: re-solve the whole constrained problem each time step, find the best legal action, execute it, measure the result, and re-solve. When the world is linear and costs are squared and force is unlimited, MPC's answer is exactly the LQR rule. When force is limited or the state hits a boundary, MPC finds the best legal action by actually checking the constraints each time. Soft penalties fail because the optimizer can trade them away; hard constraints are honoured because MPC re-tests them at every step.

The separation is clean: LQR is optimal only when constraints are absent or already satisfied with huge margin. MPC exists precisely because hard constraints matter and force matters, and the only way to handle them honestly is to check and re-solve at every step. This is why so much real-world control uses MPC — factories, rockets, rovers, data centres. The cost of re-solving is worth the guarantee of staying legal. The fact that hard constraints sit at the boundary between 'close-form LQR rule' and 'online MPC re-solve' is not accidental; it is the reason the field split into two methods.

7 · Where it is used in practice — the hard rules that a plan must never cross, and why they force re-solving instead of one-time feedback

Hard constraints gate almost every real control system: a gripper must stay clear of a shelf (never negotiable), motor torque must not exceed its rated limit (physics limit), drone rotor speed must stay below its maximum (hardware boundary), an autonomous vehicle must not enter oncoming lanes (safety), aircraft altitude must stay above terrain (survival), power-grid frequency must stay within tolerance (grid stability), an investment portfolio must stay within risk bounds (regulation). These are not soft preferences—they are lines the plan cannot cross.

A constraint is a rule written as an inequality or equation—state and action must stay within a region. A candidate plan is tested against every constraint at every time step. If any rule is violated, the plan is ruled out before its cost is even considered. A classical controller like LQR computes one feedback rule offline, assuming constraints are nowhere near violated. But when a motor caps at half the requested force, or a speed limit sits near the natural optimal speed, the constraint becomes active—the plan is right on the boundary and cannot move further. Once a constraint is active, re-solving at each step is the only clean way to handle it; LQR's fixed rule breaks because it cannot adapt to enforce the boundary.

The split between 'LQR computes once and runs forever' and 'MPC re-solves every step' is drawn exactly where hard constraints become active, because only re-solving lets you enforce an inviolable boundary.
