Gradient and First-Order Condition

A local test for whether a tiny move can immediately improve the objective.

01 · the ordinary pressure

Why this idea has to exist

An optimizer needs a direction that says which small change makes the score better or worse.

The tempting shortcut is simple: Try random changes until the cost goes down. But that shortcut breaks because random search wastes structure and becomes expensive in high-dimensional control trajectories.

02 · the object

What the math keeps track of

The controller keeps track of a gradient, derivative, or stationarity condition. This answers a practical question: use it when tiny changes to a decision have meaningful, computable effects.

The operation is concrete: Differentiate the objective and look for directions of improvement or stationarity.

03 · one concrete run

Work it through before naming the formula

A rover adjusting a steering angle can try a tiny change left and a tiny change right. If every tiny legal change makes the score worse, the rover is locally stuck. The gradient is the local slope that says which small change lowers cost fastest.

the actual math, one level deeper

For an unconstrained smooth minimum, the first derivative is zero. With constraints, the first-order condition includes which legal directions remain available.

04 · boundary

Where the idea stops working

Gradient reasoning is local and depends on smoothness and the chosen coordinates.

If that boundary is crossed, the visible failure is this: the method stops at a local stationary point that is not globally safe or useful.

Recognize it in a new problem: Use it when tiny changes to a decision have meaningful, computable effects.
