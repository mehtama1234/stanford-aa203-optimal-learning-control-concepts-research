Trajectory Optimization

Choosing an entire path of states and actions that obeys dynamics while minimizing cost.

01 · the ordinary pressure

Why this idea has to exist

Many control tasks are not about one command but about a full motion plan.

A common wrong first move is: Plan a geometric path first and worry about dynamics later. It fails when a short path may require impossible acceleration, torque, or contact transitions.

02 · the object

What the math keeps track of

The working object is a state-action trajectory. Use it when the solution is a whole planned motion, not just a feedback rule.

What the object does: Optimize the path while enforcing dynamics and constraints.

03 · read it with your hands

What to inspect first

Before naming the method, point to a state-action trajectory in the setup. Ask what a person could measure, command, update, price, or forbid in one small run.

Now apply one command or one update. Optimize the path while enforcing dynamics and constraints. After that operation, ask what changed in the next state, the path, the value, the constraint set, the policy, or the learned model. If nothing concrete changes, the explanation is still only a label.

The world check is the example: A robotic arm must move around an obstacle without exceeding joint torque or velocity limits. Try the same question on another car, drone, rover, robot arm, or reward signal. The concept should still name what changes, what is paid for, and what can fail.

04 · one concrete run

Work it through before naming the formula

A walking robot needs to move its foot from behind a box to the floor in front of it over 1.2 seconds. The answer is not one footstep point. At t = 0.0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9, and 1.2 seconds, the plan must name body angle, foot position, joint velocity, and motor torque. Suppose the foot height samples are 0.04, 0.18, 0.22, 0.12, and 0.00 meters while the box top is 0.16 meters. That looks safe at the foot. Now check the body and actuator: at t = 0.6 the torso lean is 17 degrees even though the support limit is 12 degrees, and at t = 0.9 the knee torque is 46 newton-meters even though the motor limit is 40 newton-meters. A repaired plan lifts the foot a little higher, with samples 0.04, 0.20, 0.26, 0.16, 0.00, and slows the body so torso lean peaks at 11 degrees and knee torque peaks at 38 newton-meters. The foot path may look less direct, but the whole motion is legal. Now imagine a local optimizer proposes Candidate B to save effort: lower the middle foot sample from 0.26 to 0.21 meters and reduce peak torque from 38 to 34 newton-meters. The effort score improves by 38^2 - 34^2 = 288 torque-squared units, but a midpoint check at t = 0.45 seconds predicts foot height 0.155 meters. Clearance is 0.155 - 0.16 = -0.005 meters, so the cheaper candidate clips the box and must be rejected or repaired. A start-and-finish plan would hide both failures. A picture of the foot path would hide the torso fall. Trajectory optimization treats the whole state-action history as the decision: not only where the foot goes, but what every relevant part of the robot is doing while it gets there. Now separate the plan from the tracker. Suppose the repaired nominal trajectory says torso_2 = 11 degrees at t = 0.6 and foot_height_2 = 0.26 meters. A shove during execution makes the measured state torso_2 = 14 degrees and foot_height_2 = 0.23 meters. A tracker can try to recover by adding knee torque +6 newton-meters for the next sample, but the nominal torque there was already 38, so the requested 44 newton-meters violates the 40 limit. A second recovery adds only +2 newton-meters, giving torque 40 and predicting torso_3 = 10 degrees instead of 9. It recovers more slowly but stays legal. The output is a planned motion that a feedback tracker may later follow; the trajectory itself is still a model-based promise, not proof that the real robot will stay on it after a shove. A hospital delivery robot carries a soup tray and samples its path every 0.5 seconds. Candidate A reaches the room on time and keeps tray height legal, but at t = 1.0 its acceleration is 2.4 m/s^2, above the soup limit of 1.2. Candidate B lowers acceleration to 1.0, but passes 0.03 m from a bed when the clearance rule is 0.10 m. Candidate C uses a wider path with clearance 0.14 m and acceleration 1.1, but arrives 0.4 seconds late. Trajectory optimization is the whole history check: clearance, acceleration, timing, and endpoint all have to pass. One good row cannot forgive another failed row.

the actual math, one level deeper

The decision is a sequence or curve of states and controls, such as x_0...x_N and u_0...u_{N-1}. For the five-sample walking plan, x_k might contain torso angle, foot height, joint velocity, and contact state, while u_k contains hip and knee torques. The solver minimizes a path cost such as J = sum_k [10*foot_error_k^2 + 0.01*torque_k^2] while satisfying dynamics x_{k+1}=f(x_k,u_k), boundary conditions, and constraints along the trajectory. A dynamics defect at one interval can be written defect_k = x_{k+1} - f(x_k,u_k); a physically connected plan needs defect_k = 0, not merely nice-looking samples. The legal checks are local in time: torso_lean_2 = 17 degrees violates torso_lean <= 12 degrees, and torque_3 = 46 violates torque <= 40. The repaired plan has torso_lean_2 = 11 degrees and torque_3 = 38, so those two inequalities pass; it may pay extra foot cost because 0.26 meters is higher than needed. A local surrogate step may say Delta J_effort = 0.01*(34^2 - 38^2) = -2.88, which is good for the written effort term. But constraints are not suggestions inside the score. The midpoint clearance constraint is clearance_mid = foot_height_mid - box_height >= 0. Candidate B has clearance_mid = 0.155 - 0.16 = -0.005, so its constraint residual is negative even though effort improved. A safer candidate can use foot_height_mid = 0.175, giving clearance_mid = 0.015 while still reducing torque to 36 newton-meters. Then Delta J_effort = 0.01*(36^2 - 38^2) = -1.48: less savings, but legal. The transcript's tracking point adds another check: the nominal trajectory x_bar,k,u_bar,k should itself be feasible, and the realized trajectory x_real,k may deviate from it. Tracking error can be written e_k = x_real,k - x_bar,k. In the shove example, e_torso,2 = 14 - 11 = 3 degrees and e_foot,2 = 0.23 - 0.26 = -0.03 meters. A recovery command u_track = u_bar + delta u with delta u = +6 gives 38 + 6 = 44, so torque_residual = 44 - 40 = 4 > 0. With delta u = +2, torque is 40 and torque_residual = 0, but the predicted remaining torso error is 10 - 9 = 1 degree. The tracker has not made the original plan false; it has exposed whether the plan left enough actuator room for correction. The solver may trade a slightly higher foot path for lower torque or better balance because the score and constraints apply to the whole history. The boundary is model, resolution, and tracking margin. If the grid skips the instant when the knee clips the box, if the contact model lies about foot slip, or if the nominal torques sit too close to the actuator limit, the optimized trajectory can be neat in the file and bad on the floor.

05 · boundary

Where the idea stops working

The optimized model must represent the dynamics and constraints that change the next feasible state.

When that condition fails, look for this visible break: the trajectory is visually plausible but cannot be executed.

Recognize it in a new problem: Use this term when the solution is a whole planned motion, not just a feedback rule.
