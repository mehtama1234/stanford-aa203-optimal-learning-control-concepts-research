Method Families

A method family is a response to a specific pressure: the path is too large, the future is delayed, the local model is enough, the plan goes stale, or the missing structure must be learned from data.

Read this page as a map of why the course changes tools. The switch from direct methods to dynamic programming is not a change of fashion; it is a change in what is hard. Sometimes the hard part is making a legal path. Sometimes it is pricing all futures from a state. Sometimes it is keeping a short-horizon plan from destroying tomorrow. Sometimes the missing piece has to be learned from demonstrations or reward.

The same test applies to every family: what real mistake would happen if this family did not exist?

Problem Setup

This family exists because a moving system does not obey wishes. A planner must name what is known, what can be changed, how the world moves, what future is preferred, and which lines cannot be crossed.

The move: Turn a story into state, action, dynamics, cost, horizon, constraints, and feasibility before choosing any solver.

For a drone delivery task, 'get there fast' becomes position, velocity, attitude, battery, payload, wind, rotor thrust, no-fly zones, landing pad error, and safe touchdown speed.

Optimization Foundations

Before control chooses a path, ordinary optimization teaches what it means for a choice to be locally better, blocked by a constraint, or stuck at a boundary.

The move: Look at small changes in the decision and ask whether the objective can still be lowered without leaving the legal set.

A thermostat setting may lower temperature error but hit a power limit. The gradient points toward a better setting; the constraint says whether that setting is legal.

Trajectory Optimization

A path is not a drawing between start and finish. Every point along it must be reachable from the previous point using real commands.

The move: Make the whole path the decision, enforce dynamics along the path, and ask the solver for a legal low-cost history.

A robot arm moving around a fixture needs joint angles, velocities, and torques at many times, not just a start pose and an end pose.

Dynamic Programming

Some problems are too large if the controller lists every future action sequence. The repeated structure is that after one action, the remaining future is another control problem.

The move: Attach a future-cost number to each state, then compare actions by immediate cost plus the future value of the state they create.

A rover chooses between a short rocky route and a longer smooth route by pricing wheel damage as a worse future state, not only by counting meters.

Local Structure

Near a planned motion, the full nonlinear problem may be more detail than the controller needs at every instant.

The move: Replace the local neighborhood with linear dynamics and quadratic cost, compute fast feedback, and use it only while the state stays near that neighborhood.

A drone hovering near level can push back against a small drift with LQR; after impact, the same approximation is no longer a truthful picture.

Replanning and Safety

A long open-loop plan goes stale as soon as wind, traffic, contact, or estimation error changes the state.

The move: Solve a short future problem, apply the first command, measure again, and solve from the new state while protecting the next solve.

An autonomous car replans after each small movement because nearby cars move and the measured state is more trustworthy than yesterday's prediction.

Learning-Based Control

Sometimes the model, reward, or expert behavior is too hard to write down cleanly, but data can show part of the missing structure.

The move: Fit a policy, value, reward, or model from demonstrations or experience, then keep asking what state distribution, reward, and safety boundary the learner is actually using.

A warehouse robot can clone a human drawer-opening motion, then use reward or a learned model to improve, while checking that mistakes do not move it outside the demonstrated states.
