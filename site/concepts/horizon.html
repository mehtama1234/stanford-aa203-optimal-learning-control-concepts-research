Horizon

How far into the future the controller is asked to reason.

01 · the ordinary pressure

Why this idea has to exist

Planning one second ahead and planning the whole mission are different problems.

The tempting shortcut is simple: Pick an arbitrary lookahead window. But that shortcut breaks because a short horizon can miss delayed consequences; a long horizon can be too expensive or depend on unreliable forecasts.

02 · the object

What the math keeps track of

The controller keeps track of a finite horizon, infinite horizon, or receding horizon. This answers a practical question: ask when the consequences of a bad action first appear.

The operation is concrete: Limit or extend the future cost summed by the optimization.

03 · one concrete run

Work it through before naming the formula

A delivery drone choosing its next three seconds may fly straight toward the pad. A drone planning the next thirty seconds must also budget for descent, battery reserve, and wind near the landing zone. The horizon is the length of future the controller is willing to put on the table.

the actual math, one level deeper

A finite-horizon problem chooses actions for k = 0...N. N is not just a number of samples; it decides which delayed costs and constraints can be seen by the optimizer.

04 · boundary

Where the idea stops working

The horizon should match the time scale at which consequences become visible.

If that boundary is crossed, the visible failure is this: the controller repeatedly makes greedy moves because the real cost lies just beyond the horizon.

Recognize it in a new problem: Ask when the consequences of a bad action first appear.
