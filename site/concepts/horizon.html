Horizon

How far into the future the controller is asked to reason.

01 · the ordinary pressure

Why this idea has to exist

Planning one second ahead and planning the whole mission are different problems.

A common wrong first move is: Pick an arbitrary lookahead window. It fails when a short horizon can miss delayed consequences; a long horizon can be too expensive or depend on unreliable forecasts.

02 · the object

What the math keeps track of

The working object is a finite horizon, infinite horizon, or receding horizon. Use it when you need to ask when the consequences of a bad action first appear.

What the object does: Limit or extend the future cost summed by the optimization.

03 · read it with your hands

What to inspect first

Before naming the method, point to a finite horizon, infinite horizon, or receding horizon in the setup. Ask what a person could measure, command, update, price, or forbid in one small run.

Now apply one command or one update. Limit or extend the future cost summed by the optimization. After that operation, ask what changed in the next state, the path, the value, the constraint set, the policy, or the learned model. If nothing concrete changes, the explanation is still only a label.

The world check is the example: A car changing lanes must look far enough ahead to avoid a merge conflict, not just avoid the next meter. Try the same question on another car, drone, rover, robot arm, or reward signal. The concept should still name what changes, what is paid for, and what can fail.

04 · one concrete run

Work it through before naming the formula

A delivery drone is 60 meters from the pad, flying 8 m/s, with 18 percent battery left. Each second of full-speed flight uses 2 percent battery and moves 8 meters. Each second of slow flight uses 1 percent battery and moves 4 meters. A three-second horizon sees only the next 24 meters. If the cost inside those 3 seconds is arrival progress plus battery use, full speed looks attractive: it moves 24 meters and spends 6 percent battery, leaving 12 percent. Slow flight moves only 12 meters and spends 3 percent, leaving 15 percent. But the landing zone has a crosswind band from 25 meters to 40 meters from the start, and crossing that band safely requires at least 14 percent battery so the drone can reject the gust while slowing. The short horizon ends just before the band, so it chooses the fast first command and enters the next plan with too little reserve. A thirty-second horizon sees the descent, the wind band, the battery reserve, and the need to slow before touchdown. It can choose slow flight first, not because slow is always better, but because the delayed wind cost is now visible before the first command is issued. The horizon is the length of future the controller is willing to put on the table; if the real danger appears after the table ends, the optimizer cannot price it.

the actual math, one level deeper

A finite-horizon problem chooses actions for k = 0...N. With dt = 0.5 seconds and N = 6, the controller sees only 3 seconds. With N = 60, it sees 30 seconds. The short-horizon comparison is local: full speed gives distance_3 = 3*8 = 24 meters and battery_3 = 18 - 3*2 = 12 percent; slow speed gives distance_3 = 3*4 = 12 meters and battery_3 = 18 - 3*1 = 15 percent. If the model scores only the first 3 seconds, it may prefer 24 meters of progress. The longer horizon adds the later constraint battery_at_wind >= 14 percent and the later terminal facts: touchdown speed, remaining battery, and final position. N is not just a number of samples; it decides whether delayed costs such as low battery, touchdown speed, or wind near the landing zone can enter the optimization before the first action is chosen. Too short is blind; too long can be expensive or misleading if the far future model is guessed. The practical test is the first consequence time: choose a horizon that reaches the first event that can change today's best action.

05 · boundary

Where the idea stops working

The horizon should match the time scale at which consequences become visible.

When that condition fails, look for this visible break: the controller repeatedly makes greedy moves because the real cost lies just beyond the horizon.

Recognize it in a new problem: Ask when the consequences of a bad action first appear.
